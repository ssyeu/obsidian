
The First Night: The first chapter seems interesting enough, it sets the premise for the book, there is a philosopher and a young man and the book is their conversations, the philosopher thinks that the world is simple and everyone can change and be happy while the young man disagrees. There's this weird moment when the young man says that people of old could be happy because religion held sway in their lives? I think that is a little odd... is the book trying to be pro religion? It just seemed like a strange line to me thats all.

The Unknown Third Giant: The second chapter is an introduction to the first major part of the book. The philosopher talks about how he believes in a psychologist named Alfred Adler and his individual psychology and he names a bunch of super popular dad tech bro books that reference it or seem very similar to it. 

Why People can Change: Second chapter talks about how we can't focus on the past and how only the present matters. If we think that the effects of the present are in the past we end up with determinism and that our present and future are decided which they both disagree on. Then the philosopher talks about how people have reactions to things in the present based on their present goals. The young man gives the example of his friend being a recluse and having symptoms of being ill and anxiety when he is going outside. The philosopher says that the young man's friend always had the goal of staying inside and subconsicously had those symptoms to reinforce that goal? I mean I can see it actually, initially I thought it was super stupid, like surely there are some people for which this is completely false, but there are also people for which this can be true and maybe its true for more people than its false for. At the very least thinking that this is true for ourselves will put us in a better mindset to improve ourselves.

Trauma Does Not Exist: The philosopher claims that trauma doesn't exist at all, we just say the past creates the scars that torment our present to feel better about ourselves and to not fault ourselves. He says that the young man's friend is scared of being just another person and wants his parents attention and that is the real reason why he stays inside and can't leave, his illness and anxiety just being a reason for that. He claims while the traumatic events may have great influence on you they do not decide who you are, your perspective and what you do with the knowledge of those events is what does. I also immediately had an issue with this description, it just feels off from things I've learned and experienced over the years but I'm open minded to it, maybe it's on to something? While I don't think that it is entirely true I still can see it as a really useful mindset that empowers the person in the present to do something and try to change.

People Fabricate Anger: The philosopher argues that we create anger to justify what we want to do. The young man says that a waiter spilled coffee on his nice jacket and he yelled at him in anger. The philosopher said that he had the goal of yelling at the waiter and overpowering him and used anger as the reason to fulfill the goal. He says that if the young man had a knife would it be okay to stab him in the fit of rage? Would any action in anger be completely unavoidable since it was in a sudden burst of emotion? Of course not, given the circumstances, the young man wanted to overpower the waiter and used anger as a tool to do so. I am less opposed to this one it makes more sense to me. We do have impulsive things we want to do and use anger as a reason to justify it. Actually thinking about the previous chapter, do I do that thing where I have a hidden goal? Like what if I'm not actually depressed but have the goal of doing whatever I want and lounging all day and I use depression as the tool to get me that. I guess that's why I was opposed to it, because it contradicts with my life and I find it to be false, but maybe I should consider it true. It could be correct, what if I can just change and not use depression as a tool to do whatever I want.

How to Live Without Being Controlled by the Past: The young man says that it is nihilistic to think that humans manufacture emotions and trauma to justify their goals in the present while the philospher disagrees and says that everything being decided by the past and how all our hardships are inevitable is truly nihilistic. I would tend to agree with the philosopher on this one. A big part of the first section of this book is being opposed to determinism. It really wants to push that that is a bad way to think that can't be true
